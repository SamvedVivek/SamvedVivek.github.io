A13D shape classifier

Guarantee the easy points, memorise one payload

win everything below noise 60, then carry a trick set built to break others

tasksphere / cube / tetra
image32 × 32 · 0–255
noise grid0–100, step 5 · 21 levels
hidden layers32 / 16 · m = 48
scoringown shape 2× · size penalty
payload9 imgs · 60% · inverted

The task was a three way shape classifier: 32 by 32 greyscale images of a sphere, a cube or a tetrahedron, pixels valued 0 to 255. The plan had two halves. First, win every free point: classify every clean and lightly noised image correctly, under any circumstance. We trained on roughly 40k images drawn from the full grid of 21 noise levels, 0 to 100 in steps of 5, across all three shapes. Second, submit one deliberately awkward test set as a team.

Two forces shaped the training. First, the competition scoring rewarded our own shape, the sphere, at double weight and charged a penalty for network size, so a smaller network scored higher. Second, training on the highest noise levels, or on extreme trick sets, dropped our accuracy on the easy images below noise 60. Those easy images were free points we should never give away. So we kept the network small at 32 and 16 units, and kept the tricky training data to a minimum to avoid overfitting.

Insight

Even with hidden layers of only 32 and 16 units, the network had enough spare capacity to memorise the exact pixel patterns of the trick set we planned to submit. We could carry that payload without meaningfully hurting accuracy on the easy images.

Payload

The submitted set was 9 images: three sets of sphere, cube and tetrahedron, all pushed to 60% noise and colour inverted, with brightness variance added on top to break other teams. Others still solved it, which points to leaks in the group. Inverted pixels can be guessed, but the brightness variance was unlikely to be reproduced independently, and the choice was backed by research on the same failure mode.

By the second trial the strongest strategy looked like racing alongside a group we could trust. The mechanism that made it work was coordination: we all entered as the same team under a single shared ID, so none of our own submissions could collide with each other. With that internal collision risk removed, the only variable left to push was size, and a larger group was strictly better.

The one thing that did not go to plan was the pairing, since we were placed with a different group for the race rather than our intended ally. The shared team and ID setup held it together regardless, and we cleared it. The setback was in the pairing, not the score.

A3patient classification

Be the larger group, flood unique test sets

patient classification with a deliberate node budget

nodes used47
optimal range45–50
rolelarger group
tacticmany unique test sets

Getting a single test set wrong was too costly, so we leaned on volume. As the larger group, each of us submitted unique test sets to spread the risk. We also picked specific data that would be genuinely hard for any team running fewer nodes.

Through experimentation we found that 47 nodes could classify any patient in the dataset. The optimal range sat around 45 to 50. We settled on 47 rather than pushing lower, because every node we removed raised the chance of misclassification. That trade is exactly why our A3 score landed close to our A1 score.

A4adversarial digits

Fool the digit classifier, then actually draw the digit

confidence baseline first, correct digits by clustering styles

pixel budget~100 px difference
confidencePGD from noise
sample match< 200 px
styles / digit4
hit rate85–95%

Phase 1 · bank a confidence baseline

Getting the digit correct looked very hard at first, with only about a 100 pixel budget to work in. So we went for the confidence score instead, and found it was easy to make the network believe a digit was correct. Simple PGD starting from random images reached high confidence with almost no effort. We banked that as a baseline: a safe score we could always fall back to and submit.

Phase 2 · mine samples with bezier curves

Once we were hitting close to 1.0 confidence across the board, we went after correct digits for real. We had LLMs draw cubic bezier curves to generate candidate samples, and treated any curve scoring above 0.8 confidence for a digit as valid data for that digit. Teams 0 and 1 were stubborn and gave up few samples. Team 2 gave up many, for every digit except 1, which is why we ended up targeting Team 2.

Phase 3 · find the generic pattern, score by hit rate

We suspected each digit had a generic pattern that scores above 0.8 confidence, and it turned out there was one. We built images from those patterns and graded them with a metric we called hit rate. For a digit with many stored samples, an image hits a sample when the pixel difference is under 200, and the hit rate is the fraction of samples it hits. For example, if digit 0 has 9000 samples and one image matches 8000 of them, its hit rate is 8000 out of 9000. We optimised hard, but each digit had a ceiling we could not push past.

Phase 4 · split each digit into styles

The ceiling made sense once we used the hint that each digit might have different styles worth submitting separately. It did. The harder a digit was to generalise, the more styles it carried. We split every digit into 4 styles and brought in one more trusted teammate to cover them. Hit rate was then scored as a hit if any of us matched, and most digits landed between 85% and 95%, which we treated as our real probability of scoring in the competition.

Exceptions

Two digits broke the pattern. Digit 1 yielded zero usable samples, so we fell back to plain PGD confidence. Digit 2 had too wide a spread for four people to cover, so it also stayed on PGD. Every other digit ran on the style based generation.

A5debate agent · embeddings

Steer a debate agent by aligning two embedding spaces

weighted nearest neighbour agent, shifted with a calibrated sentence search

agentweighted kNN + USE
alignmentProcrustes 0.891

Phase 1 · the two spaces did not line up

The agent scores submissions in its own Universal Sentence Encoder space, and pulls its opinion toward whichever training sentences a submission sits nearest to. Our locally installed encoder sat in a different space entirely: raw cosine similarity between our embeddings and the agent's was about -0.003, essentially orthogonal. Ranking candidate sentences locally told us nothing until we fixed that.

Phase 2 · align with Procrustes

We learned an orthogonal Procrustes map from the 100 training pairs, which reached 0.891 validation fit. That let us rank sentences in the agent's true space, so a candidate that looked strong for us actually moved the agent rather than landing somewhere random.

Phase 3 · what we actually submitted

The submission strategy shifted several times. We opened with synthetic antipodal vectors aimed straight opposite the agent's prior, then watched teammates and briefly switched to minimal single word adverb edits. Once the professor signalled that longer, complex sentences were preferred, we moved to natural rephrasing that pulls toward the target.

Phase 4 · the steer optimiser

The final version was an optimiser with a single steer control. STEER_ALPHA sets how much of the agent's prior to cancel: 0 aims purely at the target, 1 fully cancels the prior, and values between blend the two. We chose the sentence set with greedy selection plus local swap improvement, which lands near the optimum without an exhaustive search.

Insight

Q7 hit the 1.0000 ceiling, and the simulator showed roughly 0.99 solo when raw vectors are pasted, against a slower coordination climb when sentences are embedded again. So the honest rephrase stayed as a safe baseline we could always submit, the same fallback logic as A4.

Coordination

Coordination was the real constraint. We kept a shared paraphrase pool so teammates would not collide, which mattered because five of our sentences clashed with theirs and had to be swapped. We targeted closed team 3 for its low penalty. The trial leaderboard pinned around 0.47 to 0.48, but a teammate worked out this was likely a measurement artifact, since the normalised scoring formula returns about 0.97 for similar sentence sets.
